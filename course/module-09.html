Part one — the standard

What you’re doing in this module

Two things, and both are required. The W3C course teaches you the standard. Section508.gov teaches you the law that makes the standard enforceable in the country you will be working in. Neither one covers the other, and you will be asked about both.

Part one — the W3C course. “Introduction to Web Accessibility,” the most respected starting point in the field. W3C lists it at 16–20 hours across five modules: what accessibility is, people and digital technology, the business case, principles and standards and checks, and getting started. Plan two to four weeks.

The course is free. Audit it at no cost and you get the whole thing — every lesson, every quiz, all the material. That is the version we expect you to take.

You do not need to pay for anything in this module. edX will offer you a verified certificate for around $99. It is optional, and no client, agency, or procurement officer will ever ask to see it. The credential that gets you hired is in Module 10, and it is free. Buy the certificate only if you want it for yourself.
One scheduling note. W3C states the course is on edX “through at least November 2026.” They expect a version to continue long term, but if you are reading this near or after that date, check the link before you plan around it — and tell us if it has moved.

What you’ll actually learn

  • The four POUR principles in depth — Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust.
  • What WCAG “success criteria” are, and how to read one.
  • The conformance levels — A, AA, AAA — and why “WCAG 2.1 AA” is the line.
  • How disabilities, barriers, and accessibility features connect.

You don’t need to memorize the standard — you need to open it, find the relevant criterion, and understand what it asks. That’s the foundation of every audit you’ll do.

A word on pace

Twenty hours sounds like a lot until you divide it. Five hours a week finishes it in about a month; ten a week, in two. Put it on your calendar like an appointment — a steady hour most days beats one heroic weekend.

Part two — the law

Part two — the U.S. law, on Section508.gov

The W3C course is international. It will not tell you which American law applies to your client, and that is the first thing a buyer asks. This part is short — two or three hours — and it is required.

  • IT Accessibility Laws and Policies — Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act, and what it actually covers.
  • Quick Reference Guide — the short version, worth keeping open while you work.
  • Applicability & Conformance Requirements — how conformance is actually judged.
Three things to carry out of this. Section 508 covers ICT that federal agencies develop, procure, maintain, or use — which is why it reaches vendors, not just agencies. The Revised 508 Standards incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level AA by reference, and apply it to both web and non-web electronic content — that second half is why documents are in scope, and why your work exists. And agencies have been required to comply since 18 January 2018.
The part that trips people up. Section 508 references WCAG 2.0 AA. ADA Title II requires WCAG 2.1 AA. Two different laws, two different versions, both live at once. When a client asks which one applies to them, the honest answer starts with “which law are you under?” — and now you can ask it.

✦ The short version

  • Complete the free W3C course — 16 to 20 hours. Learn WCAG properly: POUR, success criteria, A/AA/AAA.
  • Read the Section508.gov law pages — two to three hours. 508 incorporates WCAG 2.0 AA and covers documents, not just websites.
  • Send two things: proof you finished the course, and your answers to the six reflection questions below.

Your reflection

Before you close this module

Everywhere else on this path, the reflection prompts are yours alone — private, unsent, nobody reads them. These six are different, and I want to be straight about that before you start writing. You send these to me. They are half of what completes the module.

Not a test. There is no answer key and I am not grading your prose. I am trying to find out how you learn, because that determines how I hand you work.

◉ The six questions

  • What did you believe about disability or accessible design before this module that you do not believe now? W3C says outright that the course “challenges the way you may approach accessibility.” Did it? If nothing shifted, say so — that is a real answer, not a failed one.
  • Which part was hardest — and was it hard because it was complex, or because it was unfamiliar? Those are different problems with different fixes, and telling them apart is a skill worth having early.
  • Where did you catch yourself skimming? Everyone skims somewhere. Naming it tells you exactly what you will have to come back for, and I would rather you knew now than in front of a client.
  • The course makes the business case — market reach, social responsibility, innovation, legal risk. Which one would you actually say out loud to a client? Which one rang hollow to you? You will be asked to make this argument for real. Better to find out now which version you can say honestly.
  • Finish both sentences: “I can now ___.” and “I still cannot ___.” The second one is more useful to me than the first, and it will never be held against you.
  • Section 508 points at WCAG 2.0 AA. ADA Title II points at 2.1 AA. When you hit that, what did you actually do? Reread it, look it up, move on, get annoyed? However you handle confusion here is how you will handle it on a client call.

A few sentences each is plenty. Write them the way you talk.

Why two of those bars are grey. Salary aggregators are unreliable for a job title this narrow. ZipRecruiter currently reports an average of $91,584 for “web accessibility specialist” while also reporting a 25th percentile of $94,000 — a figure that sits above its own average, which is not possible. Its 25th, 75th and 90th percentiles land within $500 of each other, and its top-paying cities include Nome, Alaska. That is a very small sample being stretched by a cost-of-living formula, not a picture of the market. Treat the grey bars as rumour and the purple and gold ones as fact.

Is IAAP membership worth it?

The arithmetic

The International Association of Accessibility Professionals runs the ADS, CPACC, WAS, and CPWA credentials. Individual membership is around $145 a year, and members pay roughly $100 less per exam.

  • One exam in a year: $145 to save $100. You lose $45. Membership does not pay for itself.
  • Two exams in a year: $145 to save $200. Now it does.
  • Ongoing: credential renewals run about $95 for members and more for everyone else, so once you hold something, membership keeps paying quietly.

The exam discount is the least interesting reason to join, though. The rest is what actually moves work toward you:

  • The member directory. You become findable as a credentialed professional by people specifically looking for one. For a business whose name does not explain itself, being listed where buyers already search matters more than it should.
  • Divisions and communities. Specialty groups where the people doing this work talk to each other. Subcontracting flows through those relationships — most accessibility work reaches small vendors as overflow from larger ones.
  • Credibility on a bid. "Member, IAAP" next to a credential reads as someone embedded in a profession rather than someone who took a test.
  • Free bodies of knowledge. IAAP publishes one per credential at no cost — useful whether or not you join.
The honest recommendation: do not join yet. Join the year you sit your first paid exam, when the discount and the directory start earning their keep at the same time. Confirm all current fees on IAAP's site before you budget — these change.

What automation changes — and what it doesn’t

The thing nobody wants to say out loud

You are about to invest months in a skill. You deserve a straight answer about automation rather than reassurance, so here it is in both directions.

The tools are genuinely good, and getting better. Current remediation software really does auto-tag untagged documents, repair table structure, rebuild reading order, set language and titles, and generate bookmarks. Anyone who tells a client "software cannot do this" will be corrected inside two minutes and lose the room. Do not be that person.

And it still cannot finish the job — though the honest answer is that experts disagree about how much. You will see wildly different figures quoted, and the gap is a measurement argument rather than a fight about facts.

  • Counted by WCAG success criteria — how many of the rules a machine can meaningfully check — the usual finding is 20% to 30%, with some estimates lower.
  • Counted by volume of issues found on real pages, Deque's own study reports 57%. Their argument is that criteria-counting undercounts, because the errors machines catch are also the most common ones.
  • Both can be true at once. A tool that catches most of the frequent errors can still miss most of the kinds of errors — and the kinds it misses are the judgment ones.

Read both sides and decide for yourself. That is a more useful habit than taking my word for it, and a client will eventually ask you this exact question.

Two facts worth carrying into every client conversation:

  • When the Justice Department extended the ADA Title II deadlines in April 2026, the stated reasons included “slower-than-expected technological solutions, including limits of generative artificial intelligence for remediation.” The federal government said in writing that automation is not solving this.
  • The major remediation vendors recommend human review themselves for high-stakes documents. You are not arguing against the software industry. You are agreeing with it.

What stays human

The pattern is consistent: automation handles structure, and it cannot handle meaning.

  • Alt text that carries purpose. A tool describes what an image shows — "bar chart with four blue bars." A person explains why it is in the document. WCAG asks the alternative to serve the same function as the image, and function is a judgment about context.
  • Complex tables. Merged cells, nested headers, spanning categories — software flags these and hands them back.
  • Whether a heading structure makes sense. Not "are there headings" but "can somebody find what they need."
  • Hearing the document. Reading order can be technically valid and still make no sense out loud. Only a person listening catches that.
  • Deciding what is decorative. Automation guesses. A person decides whether an image carries information.
  • Checking the machine's work. Auto-tagging introduces its own errors — mis-promoted headings, wrongly nested lists, content marked as artifact. Somebody has to verify.

The honest forecast: the floor rises and the cheap end disappears. Bulk simple remediation is already largely automated and will keep getting cheaper. What survives — and what pays — is the judgment layer, plus the accountability of a named human who signs the report. Build toward that from your first contract, and automation becomes the thing that does your boring work rather than the thing that takes your job.

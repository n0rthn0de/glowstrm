Part B

See It in Action

PART BExperiential~30 minutes

This lesson is mostly watching, and that’s the point. Reading about a screen reader is abstract; seeing someone actually use one is what makes accessibility click. Everything here is free, short, and captioned, from the most trusted sources in the field. Plan about 30 minutes; watch in order.

◈ Why this module matters

Everything else on this path teaches you what to fix. This module is where you learn who you are fixing it for. You cannot audit a website well if the person on the other side of the screen is an abstraction to you — you will find the code problems and miss the human ones. So meet them first: how they perceive a page, how they type, how they navigate, and what stops them cold. Every finding you write for the rest of your career traces back to somebody in this module.

Tools and techniques — how the work gets done

Now that you have seen the impact, here is a look at the tools and techniques practitioners actually use. You are not expected to master any of this yet — just get familiar with the shape of the work.

Tools and techniques — an introduction to how accessibility work is actually carried out. Watch on YouTube ↗

Then read all four — this is the required part.

W3C breaks the tools people use into four categories. Read every one; they are short. Together they are the clearest map that exists of how disabled people actually move through a page — and it is the map you will be testing against in Module 9.

1 · Perceptionhearing, feeling and seeing — how information reaches a person at allRead ↗2 · Presentationdistinguishing and understanding — text size, contrast, layout, clarityRead ↗3 · Inputtyping, writing, and clicking — keyboards, switches, voice, and moreRead ↗4 · Interactionnavigating and finding — moving through a site and knowing where you areRead ↗
Hold onto one distinction as you read: an assistive technology is software or hardware someone uses (a screen reader, a magnifier, a switch). An adaptive strategy is a technique someone uses (enlarging text, slowing the mouse, turning on captions). Clients mix these up constantly. You will not.

Meet nine people — this is the heart of it

W3C keeps nine short profiles of real web users. They take about a minute each, and they are the closest thing this field has to sitting beside somebody while they hit a wall. Read at least two before you go on — and notice that not one of these barriers is a coding problem until you decide to see it that way.

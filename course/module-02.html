Working for Yourself: The Basics

Working for yourself sounds either glamorous or terrifying, and it’s neither. It’s just a different setup than having a job — with different upsides and different responsibilities. Let’s take the mystery out of it.

Employee vs. independent contractor

As an employee, a company puts you on payroll. They take taxes out of each paycheck before you see it, they may offer benefits, and they direct how and when you work. As an independent contractor, you’re running your own small business. A client pays you for a result, you decide how you deliver it, and no taxes are withheld — handling those is now your job.

You’ll hear this called “1099 work,” after the tax form a client sends you (a 1099) instead of the W-2 an employer sends an employee. Same you, doing good work — different paperwork, and a different relationship to money and taxes.

The real trade: flexibility for responsibility

This is the heart of it. You gain control — your hours, your clients, your rates, where you work. In exchange, you take on responsibilities an employer used to handle: setting aside your own taxes, tracking income and expenses, finding your own work, and keeping your own records. Neither side of that trade is hidden here. The later modules and GlowStrm itself exist to make the responsibility side manageable — GlowStrm, for instance, helps with the “finding work” part.

None of this requires you to become an accountant or a lawyer. It requires you to know the categories, build a few simple habits, and know when to call a professional. That’s exactly what the next two modules set up.

Quick recap

An employee is on a company’s payroll; a contractor runs their own business and is paid for results.

“1099 work” just means contractor work — no taxes withheld, so you handle them.

The trade is flexibility for responsibility — and this course is built to make the responsibility side manageable.

Part A

Setting Up Your Business

PART ABusiness Formation~45 minutes

This is the module that takes you from “I have an idea” to “I have a registered business.” We’ll teach the landscape and the order of operations — and because the rules differ by state, you’ll do the specifics on your own state’s official portal. It’s more approachable than it sounds.

What an LLC is, and why it matters

LLC stands for Limited Liability Company. In plain terms, it puts a legal wall between your business and your personal life. If something ever went wrong with the business, that wall helps protect your personal savings, your car, your home. It also makes you look legitimate to clients and gives you a clean way to keep business money separate from personal money.

You can technically work as a “sole proprietor” with no formal setup — but then there’s no wall: you and the business are legally the same. Most people on this path start as a single-member LLC for exactly that liability protection. It’s a common, sensible starting point — but whether it’s right for you is a question for your own research and, ideally, a professional.

The general sequence

Here’s the order of operations almost every state follows. Learn it here, then do each step on your state’s portal:

Pick a business name and check it’s available in your state.

Choose a structure (most start as a single-member LLC).

File your formation paperwork with your state’s Secretary of State (filing fees commonly range from about $50 to $500).

Appoint a registered agent — who receives official mail for the business (you, or a paid service so your home address stays private).

Get a free EIN (your federal tax ID) from the IRS.

Open a separate business bank account.

Naming it — this matters more here than in most fields

In accessibility work, your name is a search term.

Here is something most naming advice will not tell you. Government buyers find vendors by typing keywords into procurement systems — eVA in Virginia, SAM.gov federally. A business name containing “accessibility” or “document” shows up in those searches. A clever abstract name does not. That is lead flow you either get for free, forever, or never get at all.

So before anything else: pick a name a buyer could stumble into.

Five things a good name does

⌕It contains a keyword“Accessibility,” “access,” “document,” or “digital” — something a buyer would actually type into a search box.
☏It survives a phone callIf you have to spell it every single time, that is a tax you pay forever. Say it out loud before you commit.
It leaves you room“PDF Remediation LLC” searches well and traps you. You will grow into audits, training, and consulting — the name should come along.
It sorts earlyVendor lists and bid tabulations are alphabetical. Early letters get seen first. Small edge, free.

The fifth: it sounds like a business, not a hobby. You will put this on invoices, contracts, and federal registrations. It should look at home there.

Check it in this order — before you fall in love

☑ Name availability checklist

Do these in order. Each one can kill a name, so kill it cheaply and early.

Three traps to avoid. Cute misspellings — Xcess, Akcess, Accessability — cost you in spelling, in search, and in credibility. Standard version numbers — “WCAG 2.1 Solutions” ages badly the moment 2.2 arrives. Near-misses with existing firms — if a company in this space already has a similar name, the confusion helps them, not you.

Using your own name

“[Your Last Name] Accessibility Services” is a completely legitimate choice, and in government work it carries a real advantage: buyers like knowing exactly who is accountable. It is also unlikely to collide with anyone else’s trademark.

The tradeoff is that a personal name is harder to sell or hand off later, and harder to grow a team behind. For a solo contractor building a track record, that tradeoff is usually worth taking.

A confession — GlowStrm breaks almost every rule above

I would rather tell you this than have you notice it yourself and wonder whether I meant any of it.

Go back up the page and score my own company against my own advice:

✕ Where GlowStrm fails its own test

Not a single one of these is an accident.

✕ No keyword. “GlowStrm” does not contain communications, strategy, accessibility, or document. A buyer searching eVA for what we sell will never surface us by name.

✕ It drops its vowels. Strm — unpronounceable on sight, and ambiguous even once you try. That is precisely the trap I told you to avoid.

✕ A capital letter in the middle. One more thing to get wrong when someone types it.

✕ It does not say what we do. You cannot derive “strategic communications” from it.

✕ It sorts at G. Middle of every alphabetical vendor list.

I spell my company name out loud on nearly every call I take. That is a real cost and I have paid it for years.

The missing vowels are the thesis

Here is the thing I did not tell you when I listed that as a failure.

GlowStrm can be read as either GlowStorm or GlowStream. We have no vowels in the latter half, so it is not clear on first glance — and I want to be straight that this is a genuine cost, not a clever escape from one.

It is pronounced glowstream. A glowing stream of clarity, sitting one letter away from the storm of noise that complicates a clear message. The name demonstrates the gap our services close. The name is our thesis; drop the vowels and you see both words at once.

So the spelling moment stopped being a tax and became the pitch. When someone asks how to spell it, I do not just spell it. I get twenty uninterrupted seconds to explain what my company is for — and they asked for them.

◈ How I actually introduce the company

“We help institutions find the gap between what they’re saying and what people are actually hearing — and close it.

We focus on institutional voice, public information, and internal communications, so the message holds up across departments instead of every office telling a slightly different story.

GlowStrm is pronounced glowstream. Think a glowing stream of clarity as opposed to a storm of noise. We help you cut through the noise.”

Read that again and count the keywords: institutions · institutional voice · public information · internal communications. Every searchable word my name refuses to carry is sitting in the sentence that follows it.

This is the actual trade. A name without keywords does not remove the work — it relocates it. You have to carry the explanation in your mouth, every time, forever. I decided that was worth it because the explanation is more persuasive than a keyword would have been. I also understand that communications work is relational, and explaining the name becomes a ritual in relationship building. You may decide otherwise, and you would not be wrong.

And what we do about the rest of it

In government contracting the name is not the discovery channel — the registration is.

This is the part worth carrying with you regardless of what you name your business. Buyers do not browse vendor names. They search by commodity code and by the description field in eVA and SAM. That is where the keywords have to live. Ours reads:

“Section 508 and WCAG 2.1 AA document remediation… PDFs, Word documents, PowerPoint presentations… tagging, reading order, alternate text, table headers… PDF/UA (ISO 14289)… accessibility audits and conformance reports.” — none of that is in our name. All of it is in our profile.

Four more things that make the trade survivable, and each one is work we actually do:

⌕We keyword-load everything but the nameeVA profile, SWaM description, SAM registration, capability statement, website copy. The searchable words live there, deliberately.
Nothing collides with usThere are dozens of firms called some arrangement of Access, Ability, and Solutions. They blur together. We are confused with no one, ever.
It left room to become thisGlowStrm went from strategic communications into accessibility without changing a single legal document. “PDF Remediation LLC” could never have launched this service line.
◈Our pipeline runs on relationshipsMost of our work arrives through a person, not a search box. A name matters less when somebody is making the introduction.

The part that actually matters for you

Here is the honest asymmetry, and I want to name it plainly rather than dress it up.

I was solving a different problem than you are. I named GlowStrm as a brand, by someone who builds brands for a living, for a company that sells through relationships I had already spent years making. I could afford to spend the search box, because I was not using it.

You are starting with no procurement history, no vendor relationships, and no reason for a buyer to know your name. The search box is the asset you have and I did not need. Spending it would cost you far more than it cost me.

So the advice above stands, and I stand outside it. Both of those are true, and the reason is the same: a name is a trade, and which trade is right depends entirely on where you are standing when you make it.

Break a rule on purpose and it is a strategy. Break it by accident and it is a bill you pay for years.

If you read all of that and still want a name that sounds like a brand instead of a keyword — do it with your eyes open. Load the description fields. Buy the matching domain. Learn to spell it cheerfully. Just know which thing you traded, and go get the relationships that have to replace it.

✦ One last thing, honestly

The name matters less than the first contract you deliver.

Nobody has ever lost accessibility work over a business name. Pick something clear, searchable, and sayable — then go do the work. You can operate under a different trade name later if you outgrow it.

Do not let this step be the reason you are still not registered three months from now.

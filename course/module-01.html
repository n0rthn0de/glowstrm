What the work pays

What it pays

Two kinds of numbers get quoted in this field, and they are not equally trustworthy. Here are both, labelled.

Published service rates — reliable. These are posted prices from a firm whose auditors hold the same DHS Trusted Tester credential you would earn here:

  • $100–250 per page for a web accessibility audit, or $1,250–2,750 for a typical whole audit.
  • From $7.50 a page for PDF remediation.
  • $195 an hour for specialist technical support.

Salary averages — treat with caution. You will see figures like $91,584 quoted for "web accessibility specialist." We looked into where that comes from, and the data is thin: the same source reports a 25th percentile above its own average, which is not possible, and lists Nome, Alaska among its top-paying cities. Salary aggregators struggle with job titles this narrow. Believe the posted service prices; treat the salary averages as rumour.

A realistic note, because honesty is the point. Most people start at the lower end and climb as credentials and real projects stack up. The ceiling is high and the floor is livable. Both things are true.

What about AI?

You should hear this now rather than three modules in, because it is the first question anyone will ask you — and the first doubt that will surface at 2am.

Yes, software is getting good at part of this. Current tools genuinely auto-tag documents, repair table structure, and rebuild reading order. Anyone who tells you otherwise will be corrected by a client in the first two minutes of a meeting.

And no, it cannot finish the job. Two facts worth carrying:

  • When the Justice Department pushed back the ADA Title II deadlines in April 2026, its stated reasons included the limits of generative AI for remediation. The federal government said in writing that automation is not solving this.
  • The remediation vendors themselves recommend human review before publishing anything high-stakes. You would not be arguing against the software industry. You would be agreeing with it.

The pattern is consistent and it is the reason this career exists: automation handles structure. It cannot judge meaning. A tool can tell you an image has no description. Only a person can say what that image is for in that document, for that reader.

The honest forecast, in one line: the cheap, bulk end of this work is being automated and will keep getting cheaper. The judgment end pays more and is not going anywhere. Why it lasts

Why the demand lasts

  • It’s remote-first — work you can do from anywhere, for clients anywhere.
  • It’s credential-driven — a certification you earn, not a network you’re born into, opens the door.
  • It’s recession-resistant — demand is created by law, not consumer mood. Companies fix accessibility because they’re required to.
  • It’s growing — the global digital accessibility market is valued at about $1.42 billion in 2025 and projected to reach $3.24 billion by 2034, a compound growth rate of 8.6%. North America holds roughly 36% of it (Straits Research).

And the pressure is rising in real time. Federal digital-accessibility filings hit roughly 2,000 in the first half of 2025 — a 37% jump over the same period the year before, and the full year closed with more than 5,000 cases across federal and key state courts (UsableNet). Every filing is an organisation that now has to pay someone to fix what it shipped. That someone is increasingly people like you.

The throughline

This is a field where effort and credentials outweigh pedigree and connections — which is exactly why it works as a path to economic mobility. You are not asking anyone for a seat. You are earning a credential the market already needs.
